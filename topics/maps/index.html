🎲 I Wanted to Play Star Frontiers Again. I Accidentally Built a Game Engine.

It started with a photo of the 1983 Star Frontiers Referee’s Screen on Instagram. Four browser games and roughly two thousand lines of JavaScript later: a shared engine where a new module is content plus a config object, dice rendered with real per-type silhouettes in CSS clip-path, and every sound synthesized in Web Audio with no files to host. The fourth game, Railroad Valley: Nine Litres, runs on genuine USGS topo tiles of Nye County, Nevada — 37 locations on real coordinates, walking times computed from terrain pace plus a Naismith climb penalty, and a water model where 38°C costs 1.33 litres an hour walking against the nine litres you started with. Also three failures documented in public: dice checks that could never succeed because the modifier spread was wider than the die, atmosphere written where information was needed, and death clocks the player could not see. The standing fix was tools/audit-dice.js, which boots every game headlessly and flags impossible checks — it has caught five real bugs, two of them already live.

🌲 Finding the Trees

A stretch project into airborne LiDAR and point clouds — PDAL pipelines, ground classification, canopy height models, individual tree detection — built end to end from a synthetic Finnish boreal stand through to real national data. The detector scored 78.4% recall against 450 trees whose positions I set myself, then recall rose as the point cloud thinned, a +0.00 m ground calibration concealed a canopy bias, two definitions of mean height landed on opposite sides of the truth, and a 100% harvest-targeting precision turned out to be structurally guaranteed. Validated against 1,295 stands from the Finnish Forest Centre inventory: height to within 1.19 m at r = 0.962, roughly one stem in six recovered.
