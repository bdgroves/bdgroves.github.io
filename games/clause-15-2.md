# Clause 15.2

*This is the story underneath [Adjuster's Report](/games/adjusters-report.html). You can play it without any of this — and you probably should first, because the game hides most of it behind seven acts and a dice roll. But a player only ever meets it in pieces, so here it is whole.*

*It contains everything. Don't read it first.*

---

## The brief is four lines

> Chief engineer Aurel Ganz, decompression event in the reactor annex, Station Corvid-9 Relay. Death ruled accidental by on-site medic. **Confirm and close.**

Confirm and close. Not investigate.

The policy pays eleven million dollars on misadventure and nothing at all on an act by a covered party, and everybody who wrote that brief knows which answer they're hoping you bring home.

There's one more thing worth holding onto before you land, and it's the thing that shapes everything after: **Corvid-9 Relay Holdings is in administration.**

Whatever this policy pays doesn't go into anybody's pocket. It goes to creditors, in order, and the people on that station see none of it.

Which rules out the simplest motive and leaves the ones that are harder to look at. A licence. A career. A station that closes tidily instead of collapsing.

Nobody out here is killing anybody to get rich. That's never what it is.

---

## The room has been cleaned

Eleven hours between the alarm and your arrival, and somebody used them.

That's a finding in itself, and it isn't proof of anything. Plenty of honest people tidy up after a death because looking at it is unbearable.

The annex gives you two things if you work it: scuff marks in the seam where the deck plate meets the hatch frame, two sets, one not Ganz's size. And a suit whose service tag says the seal was cycled and certified, and whose neck ring says otherwise — oxidation banding four cycles deep, undisturbed. Somebody signed a form they didn't do the work for.

Records gives you three more. A fourth badge cycle on the annex hatch that shift, stripped of its identifier by somebody who knew how but not quite well enough. A death certificate that puts time of death nineteen minutes before the pressure alarm fired, which is impossible in the direction that matters. And a filing sitting unsent in Ganz's outbound queue, addressed to the regulator, subject line reading only: **ANNEX — THIRD REQUEST.**

Third request. He'd asked twice before and filed neither. A man doesn't get to a third request quietly.

## The document nobody sends the adjuster

Your copy of the policy runs to six clauses and a schedule reference.

A policy of this size does not run to six clauses.

The real thing is forty-one pages and it's filed on the station because it has to be, and pages thirty-four to thirty-nine are the part nobody forwards. Three clauses your brief left out — which may be laziness, and usually is — and one endorsement.

**Endorsement 4.** Effective six weeks before the death.

> The Schedule is amended to add AUREL GANZ as a Scheduled Person under clause 3.1, with a Limit of Indemnity of USD 11,000,000.

Until six weeks ago, Aurel Ganz was worth nothing to this policy at all. Somebody made him worth eleven million, and then he died in the annex he was about to file a report on.

And the three missing clauses turn out to be the useful ones:

**9.1 — Right of Access.** They cannot seal a record from you, and specifically cannot seal one after notification of a claim.

**12.4 — Duty of Disclosure.** A covered party who withholds a material fact forfeits the benefit of the policy *in respect of themselves*. Not the company's cover. Theirs.

**15.2 — Material Alteration.** No endorsement takes effect unless countersigned by an officer holding relevant authority, *whose designation shall be recorded on the endorsement*.

Somebody has to sign their job title next to it.

Somebody did.

---

## Three people, none of whom want money

**Administrator Cass Holt** filed the incident report inside two hours and is very keen you find it straightforward. Ganz's safety filing would have closed the annex, and closing the annex closes the station. Forty people, final wages, and an administrator who'd rather it ended tidily than collapsed.

**Junior Engineer Dov Marek** hasn't slept. He answers every question with a question and looks at the floor doing it. Ganz put a formal incompetence note in his file eleven days before, and it would have followed him off-station for good.

**Dr. Ilsa Vann** signed the death certificate in under an hour, perfectly composed and slightly too helpful. Ganz had opened an inquiry into her dispensing records. A licensing board would not have made the distinction between sloppy and criminal.

Each of them had the authority to countersign that endorsement, and for a different reason. Holt signs off on everything. Key-person cover requires somebody to certify the person is operationally key — with Ganz away, that was Marek. And it requires a medical sign-off on insurability, which is Vann by definition.

**The culprit is drawn fresh every time you take the contract.** All three motives have to work, and they do, because none of them is greed.

---

## How you tell

The innocent ones give you the unflattering truth.

> "I did not like him and I am not sorry he is gone and I did not touch him, and all three of those are true at once."

That's a much harder sentence to say than a denial. People constructing a story rarely construct one that makes them look that bad.

The guilty one gives you something clean and rehearsed, and when pressed says: *"If you have something, produce it."*

Which is what a person says when they've already worked out what there is to produce.

And when you put clause 15.2 on the table with your finger under the designation line — *an endorsement is void unless an officer countersigns it and records their designation; somebody did; it's typed; it's your job title* — they say:

> "That is not — that is an administrative signature. That is not what you are turning it into."

They don't say it's wrong.

## The dead man's computer

His station account is closed and his queue was purged on the standard decedent cycle, which is routine and which three people tell you.

But a personal partition isn't purged on the decedent cycle. A personal partition isn't purged at all, because nobody ever wrote a procedure for it and nobody wants to be the one who does.

It's sitting there. It's locked.

There's no code to guess and you wouldn't guess it if there were. There's a clause, and an argument, and a man who makes the opposite argument well.

You invoke 9.1 — *all records howsoever held* — and Holt argues that a decedent's personal partition is the property of the estate, not a record of the Policyholder, and that the clause doesn't reach it.

**He is probably right.**

He opens it anyway, under protest, and records the protest. Or you can just *ask*, with no clause and no leverage, and he opens it because asking was easier than arguing — which is not a legal basis and both of you know it, and which comes back at you in eighty days as a bad faith allegation you handed them yourself.

Inside is thirty gigabytes of a person.

## What Ganz was actually doing

Seven drafts of the same filing, across five weeks. Draft one is four paragraphs and extremely polite. Draft four is eleven paragraphs and is not. Draft seven is back to four and polite again, and it's the one he queued.

The line he cut, from draft four:

> *"I am aware that closing the annex closes the station. I am aware what that costs and who it costs. I am asking anyway because the alternative is that I sign something saying it is safe."*

He knew exactly what his filing would do to forty people. He filed it anyway, politely, and took out the part where he said so.

There's a folder called ORCHID with eleven photographs of a twenty-eight foot sailboat he hadn't bought yet, and a spreadsheet working out that two more rotations covers it outright. That's not evidence and you couldn't put it in a finding. **A man who is afraid does not spend his evenings looking at boats.**

An unsent message to a sister in Tempe, dated the day before: *"Tell Mum I'll call Sunday. I keep saying that. I will though."*

And his own annotated copy of the policy — because chief engineers read the liability policy, being the ones who get named in it. Mostly ordinary notes. *check limit. does this cover contractors. ask Holt re: annex exclusion.*

And then, on page thirty-six, beside Endorsement 4:

> **"When was this added? Nobody told me I was scheduled. Ask."**

Timestamped four days before he died.

His calendar has a forty-minute meeting four days before the annex. No attendees listed. Titled only: *ask re: sched.*

**Marked as completed.**

He asked somebody. That somebody is on this station and has spoken to you today.

## Ninety days

Clause 11.6 gives the Policyholder ninety days to challenge your finding on grounds of manifest error or bad faith.

On day eighty-one a forty-page bundle arrives from a firm in Reno, and it does not argue that you're wrong.

> The Policyholder respectfully submits that the Adjuster's finding discloses **manifest error** within the meaning of clause 11.6, in that the evidential basis recorded under clause 14.3 is insufficient to support a finding adverse to indemnity on the balance of probabilities.

They're not saying you named the wrong person. They're saying you didn't do the paperwork.

Those are different accusations and only one of them is survivable.

You get one filing to answer it. You rest it on the endorsement, or on the victim's own annotation, or on the contradiction, or on the weight of what you established. Or you withdraw, and amend to misadventure, and eleven million clears inside a fortnight, and nobody ever asks you anything again.

---

## The one to sit with

There's an outcome where the paperwork is immaculate and the challenge fails on the paperwork.

And it was somebody else.

A finding can be procedurally perfect and still be the wrong answer, and yours is, and it is now determinative as between the parties.

That's what clause 11.6 means when nobody is watching.

---

## The joke the whole game rests on

The clause that catches a killer is not the exclusion.

It's clause 15.2 — a piece of administrative housekeeping requiring that whoever signs an amendment types their job title next to it.

Nobody drafted that to catch anybody. Somebody drafted it because an endorsement got countersigned by the wrong person once, and it cost an insurer money, and a lawyer added a sentence to a template.

A technicality catches them, and the technicality was never meant for this.
