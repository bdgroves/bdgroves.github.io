# Forty-One Entries

*This is the story that sits underneath [Railroad Valley: Nine Litres](/games/stay-with-the-vehicle.html). You can play the game without any of it. But a player only ever meets this in fragments, out of order, while dying of thirst — so here it is in one piece, in the order it happened.*

---

## I.

Raymond Hollis was a lineman.

Thirty-one years with a rural electric co-op out of Ely, the last nine as a crew foreman. Poles, transformers, storm callouts at two in the morning on roads with no names. Six foot two, hands permanently marked around the nails, and a habit of wearing gloves for work and never for anything he cared about.

On his days off he dug bottles.

It's a real hobby and a very Nevada one — men with a shovel and a probe rod working the dumps of dead mining camps, looking for purple glass and cobalt and embossed medicine bottles that somebody threw away a hundred years ago. He'd been doing it since his thirties. He had a garage full of it.

Both facts matter. The bottles are why he was in Railroad Valley at all. The trade is why he couldn't let go of what he found there.

---

## II.

**April 2001.**

> FTF. Drove down from Ely for the Dutchman dump, got skunked, found this instead. Good spot. Left a compass. — R.H.

He was looking for glass. He found a geocache — a .30 cal ammo can under a cairn on a point above the valley, placed a few months earlier by somebody he'd never meet. First to find.

**September 2001.**

> Dana with me. She had it in four minutes and I had it in twenty-five and I am not going to hear the end of this. Two amber, one aqua out of the dump below. — R.H.

She's thirteen. She's beaten her father at his own hobby, in public, in writing, and he's the one who wrote it down.

This is the only entry in forty-one that's about a person.

**December 2001.**

> Cold, no wind. Walked the fence line out of habit. Spoil along it for about a mile, backfilled rough, no settling yet. Somebody trenched this in the last year or two. Nothing on it above ground.

A civilian sees disturbed ground. Ray sees *spoil*, and sees that somebody backfilled it in a hurry, and notes that nothing was put on top of it.

He isn't investigating anything. He's filling time on a cold day, in the one subject he happens to know everything about.

**March 2002.**

> Antelope on the playa first light, forty head. Dug the east dump, mostly clear glass, one good cobalt base. Trench still bothering me.

Three sentences: the country, the hobby, and the thing. In that order, for the last time.

---

## III.

There's no logbook entry for this part. There wouldn't be.

Sometime in 2002 he's sitting in an old adit out of the sun with a crate of bottles that are too common to keep and too good to throw away. There's a bag of mortar in the truck, because there's always a bag of mortar in a lineman's truck.

He sets a dozen bases into the wall to see what it looks like.

Three feet of it. Spacing all over the place. Two cracked and left in. Brown mixed with green, because he hasn't yet decided that sorting is the point.

Pencilled on the mortar beside it, in block capitals:

**FIRST TRY — 2002**

And under that, smaller:

**BETTER NEXT TIME**

---

## IV.

**June 2002.** The hinge of the whole thing.

> Walked the trench line 2.6km with the probe. It does not go to the site. It runs past it, parallel, sixty yards this side of the fence. No riser, no pedestal, no marker tape in the spoil. You do not lay cable and not flag it. Nobody lays cable and not flags it.

There is a real satellite calibration site out on that playa. Instrument masts, a solar array, a placard explaining in small type that satellites are aimed at this specific piece of ground to check whether their instruments still tell the truth. Openly labelled. Entirely above board. One of the most reassuring things in the valley.

The trench doesn't go to it. It goes *past* it.

And look at what he does with that. He doesn't speculate. He states what it violates. *You do not lay cable and not flag it.* A man protecting the standards of his own trade against something that has ignored them.

**September 2002.**

> BLM office in Ely. No permit on file, no work order, girl at the counter could not have been nicer and had no idea what I was describing. Called the co-op too. Not ours.

He checked with his own employer. It isn't theirs.

**November 2002.**

> Bad night, slept in the truck. Ran the AM band for something to do. Carrier on 1530. No modulation, no ID, no fade. It is still there at four in the morning.

**April 2003.**

> 1530 again. On four minutes, off eleven seconds, on four minutes. Timed it two hours, never varied by more than a second. That is not a fault. Faults drift.

*That is not a fault. Faults drift.*

A tradesman ruling something out is more frightening than a believer ruling something in. He is not saying it's mysterious. He's saying he has eliminated the boring explanation, professionally, with a watch.

He never writes *which means*. Not once in four years.

---

## V.

**January 2004.**

> White pickup on the rise when I came out. Sat the whole time I was at the cairn. Left when I left. No plate I could read at that range.

**March 2004.**

> Two today. I waved. One of them waved back, which I did not expect and have thought about since.

That's the most important line in the logbook.

In four years and twenty-six entries, Ray writes something that isn't a fact exactly twice. Both times it's about being seen.

Whatever this is, it isn't hostile. Which is worse.

**May 2004.**

> Dug the line at the coordinates below. Grey conduit, thumb gauge, and a sealed enclosure spliced in at a pull point. Stub antenna. Inventory tag, letter-dash-seven-and-year, same format we use. Not our numbers. Photographed it and put the spoil back the way I found it.

He knows exactly what he's looking at. That's the problem. He's stuck a thousand of those tags on a thousand pieces of kit.

And he fills the hole back in. Of course he does.

**August 2004.**

> Film came back 24 blanks. Could be the lab. Taking the good camera in September and doing it properly.

He offers the mundane explanation himself. That's what makes it land.

Then, at the bottom of the page, underlined:

> **1530 all night now. Not a station.**

And a set of coordinates.

The logbook stops here. Forty-one entries, twenty-six of them his, handwriting getting smaller across four years — not deterioration, a man fitting more into a space he doesn't want to run out of.

---

## VI.

**September 2004.**

He parks the Ford under the Pancake Range, thirteen kilometres west of the dig site, out of sight of every rise that matters. A man who has been watched by unmarked pickups for two years has learned to park somewhere else.

It's also why there's water cached across that whole valley in jugs under slabs — not emergency supplies. A supply line, for a man who walks everywhere now, on purpose.

Shovel and probe rod in the bed. Four sealed jerry cans. Keys in the ignition. A hat on the passenger seat. The driver's seat pushed all the way back.

And tucked into the visor, a photograph gone almost white: a girl of about thirteen, squinting into the sun, holding up an ammo can with both hands like a fish she caught.

He walks the trench line two kilometres north of the box.

His last written line, in a notebook in the glovebox:

> **There is a lid.**

He was fifty-one.

---

## VII.

There is a lid.

A steel plate set flush in the playa, a metre square, recessed handle, and no lock at all — which is somehow the most alarming detail available.

Sixty rungs down it opens into a corridor with a poured floor and a strip light running the length of it, and the strip light is **on**. Cable trays overhead. Junction boxes at intervals with inventory stickers, some carrying recent years. The floor is swept — not clean, *swept*, with a broom-shaped absence of dust and a faint arc where somebody works from a fixed point and turns.

There's an alcove off it with a steel desk, a chair pushed in square, a telephone with no dial tone, and a clipboard. The clipboard is a maintenance schedule, ruled columns, initials and dates, and the most recent line was signed off four weeks ago.

There's a wall calendar from a valve manufacturer in Sparks, Nevada. 1987. Turned to August and never turned again. Nobody has taken it down in thirty-nine years, and somebody wipes the desk under it.

And there's a tap. Plumbed, pressurised, potable water, sixty rungs under a valley where people die of not having any.

---

## VIII.

Forty metres along, the wall changes.

Bottle bases set into painted bands, sorted by colour — brown at the floor, through green and clear, to a narrow course of cobalt at head height. Blue glass is rare in a dump because blue glass was expensive. He kept every base he found instead of selling them, which cost him real money, and put them all in one band where they'd catch a light best.

He used the corridor's own industrial floor enamel as his base coat. He didn't carry materials down a ladder. He worked with what was there.

And it's better than the mine. Obviously better. Even spacing. Clean colour steps. Not one cracked base in sixty metres, which means he was carrying them down a ladder one at a time.

Whatever he learned in twenty years of doing this, he put it here. In the place nobody he knew would ever see it.

Where a base has fallen out there's a stencilled corridor designation and an arrow pointing north. He set a bottle over the letters and left the arrow showing.

---

## IX.

The work stops sixty metres along. Mid-band, in the middle of a colour, the way work stops when the person stops.

On the floor: a headlamp, dead, switch still forward. A canvas jacket, folded — folded, not dropped. A supermarket receipt in the pocket dated September 2004. And a notebook, mostly blank, with one last written page:

> **They know I come down here. Nobody has ever said anything. I think they like it.**

That's not a man who believes something otherworldly admires his art.

That's a tradesman who spent thirty-one years being the guy who fixes things for people he never meets, and who has decided he's been given permission — because permission, in his world, has always looked exactly like nobody stopping you.

The corridor goes on north past the last bottle. Lit. Swept. No door, no sign, for further than anyone is willing to walk.

We don't find out.

---

## X.

The batteries in the cache are dated 2019.

Not twenty-two years old. About ten. Nobody has signed the logbook since 2004, but somebody has been maintaining the can.

Taped inside the lid, under the card that explains geocaching to people who find these by accident, there's a second card. Newer lamination. Printed rather than handwritten — deliberately, so it would last.

> This cache belonged to my father. He put it here in 2001. He came out here a lot. I came with him sometimes.
>
> He stopped coming home in September 2004. He was 51. His truck was never found. Neither was he.
>
> I come out when I can and change the batteries. I have never signed the log. He signed it. I would rather the last name in it stayed his.
>
> If you knew him, or you found something of his, there is a number on the back.

You turn it over. The lamination has lifted along that edge and the last four digits have gone to a grey smear.

She came constantly from 2005 to 2009, because she was seventeen and thought she was going to find him. Twice a year through college. She missed 2015 — she got married, it was a good year, and she's felt bad about it since. Nobody went anywhere in 2020.

And at some point she stopped coming in September, and never made a decision about it.

She comes in the spring now.

---

## XI.

That's where you come in, twenty-two years later, with a Jeep that won't start and nine litres of water.

You retrace him backwards and out of order, while dying. The coordinates reach you as a voice reading twelve digits in the gap on 1530 at two in the morning, rather than as a line in a book. You find the truck before you find the man who parked it. You drink his water.

She says his truck was never found.

You know where his truck is.

And when you've read her card, you can sign the log. The golf pencil is worn down to about forty millimetres and it still writes, because a pencil doesn't care how long it's been in a box.

You write the date. Where the Jeep is. That you took water and you're sorry and you'll replace it if you can.

Then the trench, the frequency, the lid, and where the lid is — because it's the only thing you have that she asked for.

And at the top of the page, in capitals, because it's the only part of this that will matter to one person:

> **HIS TRUCK IS AT 38°22.4′ N 115°52.6′ W. THE KEYS ARE IN IT.**

Then the book goes back in the bag, the bag in the can, the can under the rocks, the way he did it, the way she does.

Forty-two entries.

---

## XII.

You might not get out. That's left open on purpose — it's presumably what happened to him.

But the book is in the can and the can is under the rocks, and she comes in the spring.

She'll lift them off and find a forty-second entry in handwriting that is not hers and is not his.

---

*He was a lineman who dug bottles on his days off, and he found a trench that wasn't on any print, and he followed it for two and a half years because that's what you do with a loose end — and somewhere along the way he started making the most beautiful thing he ever made in a place he could never show to anybody. Including, in the end, his daughter.*
