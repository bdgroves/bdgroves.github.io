Part one

The license, step by step

There are three US license classes, and each one builds on the last. General is the one that opens the long-distance HF bands, but you can't skip to it: you need a Technician license first. The good news is that most exam teams will let you take the Technician and General exams back to back at the same sitting, so you can walk in with nothing and walk out a General.

Element 2

Technician

35 questions · pass with 26

Radio rules, safety, and how repeaters and antennas work. Opens all of VHF and UHF (local repeaters, the ISS, FM satellites) plus a little HF: Morse and data on 80, 40 and 15 m, and voice on 10 m.

Question pool: 2026–2030, in use since July 1, 2026

Element 3 · the goal

General

35 questions · pass with 26

Propagation, HF operating, station setup and a bit more electronics. Opens most of every HF band, which is how you talk across oceans with a modest antenna.

Question pool: 2023–2027, good through June 30, 2027

Element 4

Amateur Extra

50 questions · pass with 37

The full band edges, where much of the rare-country hunting happens, and a lot more theory. A goal for later.

Needs General first

What it costs

  • $35 FCC fee for a new license, paid online after you pass. It covers you for ten years.
  • $0–15 for the exam session, depending on the volunteer team. Some are free.
  • Upgrading from Technician to General doesn't carry a new FCC fee.
  • Study materials can be free. HamStudy.org and the KB6NU No-Nonsense guides cost nothing.

How to study

  • My ham license cheat sheet: the rules, band edges, formulas and traps for both exams on one page, in the same style as the GISP sheet.
  • HamStudy.org: flashcards through the real question pool, then practice exams. When you score 85% or better three times running, book the exam.
  • KB6NU's No-Nonsense study guides: short, plain-English explanations of every question.
  • The ARRL General Class License Manual if you'd rather learn the why, not just the answers.
  • The Radio Club of Tacoma runs General classes and exam sessions, or search sessions at hamstudy.org/sessions.

    Part two

    What General opens up

    HF, the shortwave bands, is where signals bounce off the ionosphere and come down a continent away. Each bar is one band, drawn to its own scale. Filled parts are yours with a General license; the hatched parts are kept for Extra class. Flip to Technician to see how much General adds.

    Morse and data (FT8, digital) Voice and image Extra only Where FT8 lives: listen here first

    General also gets the five 60 m channels and everything from 6 m up. Band edges from the FCC Part 97 limits for ITU Region 2. Power and mode limits have more detail than a chart can show; check the ARRL band plan before you transmit.

    Part three

    Reaching far away

    Pinging the far side of the world is real and it's easier than it sounds. A few watts and a wire antenna can be heard in Japan, and some of the tools will show you exactly where you landed.

    See who heard you

    FT8 + PSK Reporter

    FT8 is a digital mode that pulls signals out of the noise. Call CQ for fifteen seconds and, a minute later, PSK Reporter puts a dot on a map for every station that decoded you. It's the closest thing ham radio has to a ping.

    A beacon that maps the sky

    WSPR

    WSPR ("whisper") sends a tiny beacon, a fraction of a watt, and listeners worldwide report it to WSPRnet. Leave it running overnight and you get a map of where the bands opened and when.

    Ride the terminator

    The grey line

    Around sunrise and sunset the ionosphere's lower, signal-eating layer fades before the upper, reflecting layer does. Paths that run along the grey line on the map above can reach places that are silent the rest of the day.

    Radio outdoors

    Parks on the Air

    POTA is operating from parks with a portable station. It mixes hiking with radio, and the Radio Club of Tacoma runs a monthly club activation you can tag along on.

    Keep score

    QSLs and DXCC

    Contacts are confirmed through ARRL's Logbook of the World. Confirm 100 countries and you earn DXCC, the long-standing badge of a long-distance operator.

    Why it changes

    The sun's cycle

    Sunspots make the ionosphere a better mirror. Solar Cycle 25 peaked around 2024–25, so 10 and 15 m are still lively and 20 m is the dependable workhorse. 40 and 80 m come alive at night.

    Part four

    Talking to space

    This is the part a Technician license already covers. The ISS carries ham radios, and a dual-band handheld with a small handheld antenna is enough to work them as the station passes over. Each pass lasts about ten minutes.

    Working out the orbit…

    ISS · NA1SS · Technician

    Voice repeater

    Uplink
    145.990 MHz, tone 67.0
    Downlink
    437.800 MHz

    Talk up on 2 m, listen on 70 cm. Say your call sign and grid, keep it short, and let others in. On the downlink, tune 437.810 at rise, 437.800 overhead and 437.790 as it sets to follow the Doppler shift.

    ISS · RS0ISS · Technician

    APRS packet

    Frequency
    437.825 MHz

    Send a short text packet with your position and see it relayed from orbit on ARISS trackers. ARISS lists it as active but still being tested.

    ISS · anyone can listen

    SSTV pictures

    Frequency
    145.800 MHz (events only)

    A few times a year the ISS sends slow-scan TV images. Any radio that hears 145.800 and a phone app decodes them, no license needed. Watch the ARISS status page for event dates.

    Satellite · SO-50 · Technician

    FM satellite

    Uplink
    145.850 MHz, tone 67.0
    Downlink
    436.795 MHz

    One of the longest-running FM birds and a good first satellite. Check AMSAT's live status before a pass; small satellites come and go.

    The ISS radios go quiet for spacewalks, dockings and crew schedules. ARISS posts what's on at its status page (last checked October 31, 2026, when the voice repeater was in service). ARISS also sets up scheduled school contacts with the crew, which clubs help run.

    Shack talk

    The phonetic alphabet, the Q codes everyone uses, and the shorthand you'll hear on the air the first night.
