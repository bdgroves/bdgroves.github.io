🏁 The Dakar in Miniature: The Algarve, the Atlas and the Sahara

October 30, 2026 Brooks Groves 11 min read
Dakar Rally Morocco Motorsport Travel 3D Three.js Open Data

The Dakar Rally is going back to Africa. For seven editions it has run in Saudi Arabia, and the 2027 route there was announced back in May. Then on 25 October it was dropped over security in the Middle East, and five days later ASO came back with a whole new plan: a prologue in Portugal’s Algarve on 9 January, a ferry across to Morocco, and twelve stages through the Atlas and the Sahara to a finish in Marrakech on 23 January. It’s the first Dakar on African ground since 2007.

I’ve been following the rally on my Dakar 2027 tracker, and I’d just finished my little tabletop dioramas of Seattle, Yosemite and the Amalfi Coast. So the day the route came out, the obvious question was: could the same engine hold a whole rally? It can. Here is the 49th Dakar, in miniature.

From the Algarve to Africa

The rally starts in Europe. The prologue on Saturday 9 January is a short 29 km loop around Loulé and Faro that sets the starting order. Stage 1 then heads east toward the Strait of Gibraltar, and that night the whole caravan of bikes, cars, trucks and support crews boards the ferry for Nador on Morocco’s Mediterranean coast. The next day is an untimed transfer, almost 600 km down to the first bivouac at Bouârfa near the Algerian border. On the board the ferry crossing is the dashed white line, and if you ride along you can watch your bike roll onto the boat.

Europe and Africa, 14 km apart at the Strait of Gibraltar. The dashed line is the ferry to Nador.

🏁 History

How the Dakar left Africa, and came back

The rally began as the Paris–Dakar in the winter of 1978–79. Thierry Sabine had got lost in the Libyan desert on a motorbike the year before and decided other people should get to feel the same thing. For thirty years it ran from Europe across the Sahara to Senegal. In January 2008 the whole event was cancelled the day before the start, after threats against the route through Mauritania. The name stayed, but the rally moved to South America from 2009 to 2019 and then to Saudi Arabia from 2020. Saudi Arabia is due to host the 50th edition in 2028, so 2027 is a one-year homecoming, and it still doesn’t reach Dakar itself.

The Atlas in January

Morocco’s spine is the High Atlas, and in January it carries snow. Jbel Toubkal, at 4,167 m (13,671 ft) the highest peak in North Africa, stands just south of Marrakech. The route crosses the range more than once: stage 6 from Zagora to the coast at Agadir, and the long run north on stage 11 from Smara to the finish. Fly in from the north and the whole wall rises out of the green Haouz plain, white along its crest.

The High Atlas from above Marrakech: green plain on this side, the Sahara on the other.

🪨 The science

One mountain range, two climates

The Atlas was squeezed up as Africa pushed slowly north into Europe, crumpling an old rift basin that had opened as the Atlantic was being born. The same squeeze helped raise the Rif and Spain’s Sierra Nevada, both on the board too. The mountains also split the country’s weather. Winter storms roll in off the Atlantic from the west and northwest and drop their rain and snow on the near side. What’s left by the far side is dry air, and that rain shadow is a big part of why the Sahara starts so suddenly south of the crest. I used that rule to color the diorama: the farther south a place is, and the more mountain stands between it and the Atlantic, the drier it gets.

Into the desert

From Bouârfa the rally runs two big loops out into the high, stony steppe, then stage 4 cuts west through the dunes at Merzouga to Zagora, on the Draa River. That’s where the palms are. The Draa valley is one long ribbon of date groves and earth-walled villages running down to the edge of the Sahara, and on the board it’s the thin green thread through the sand. Zagora gets a loop of its own, 365 km of special, before the rally turns for the coast.

Riding along with the bikes near Zagora, the Draa oasis on the right.

🏜️ The science

Erg, reg and hamada

Most people picture the Sahara as one big sea of sand, but only about a fifth of it is dunes. Geographers have three words for the ground out there. An erg is a sand sea, like Erg Chebbi at Merzouga, where the dunes stand up to about 150 m tall. A reg is a wide plain of gravel, polished by the wind. A hamada is bare rock plateau, often dark with desert varnish. The diorama has all three: orange ergs with ripples drawn across the wind, pale regs, and darker hamada. Rally crews know them by feel. Hamada wrecks tires, regs let you fly, and ergs are where the navigators earn their keep.

Agadir, and the long way south

Stage 6 crosses the Anti-Atlas to Agadir on the Atlantic for the rest day on Sunday 17 January. Then the real test begins. Stage 7, from Agadir south to Smara, has the longest special of the rally: 500 km against the clock. From Smara, stage 8 runs out to a refuge bivouac in the desert for the marathon night, and stage 9 comes back. Smara gets one more loop, 470 km, before stage 11, the longest day: 1,035 km north across both Atlas ranges to Marrakech. A short 22 km special on 23 January finishes it.

The cars leave the Agadir bivouac for stage 7, the longest special of the rally.

🔧 How the rally works

The marathon night

Most nights a rider comes into the bivouac and hands the bike to a team of mechanics who work until dawn. On a marathon stage there are no mechanics. The riders and crews sleep at a bare refuge camp and fix whatever broke themselves, with the parts and tools they carried, before setting off again in the morning. A cracked radiator or a worn-out clutch that would be a quick swap anywhere else can end a rally on the marathon night. On the board the refuge pin is dark red.

The islands off the edge

The board runs far enough west to catch two island groups the rally never visits: Madeira, and the Canary Islands, with Teide on Tenerife rising to 3,715 m, the highest point in Spain. They’re there because the box had to be that big to hold the route, but I’m glad they are. Under the see-through sea you can see how they stand on the ocean floor: volcanoes rising from deep water, nothing like the continental shelf along the coast.

The Canaries, with Teide on Tenerife, and the desert coast in front.

Night in the bivouac

Slide the clock to Night and the board goes dark except for the cities, the lanterns in each bivouac and the headlights of whoever is still out on the route. That’s the Dakar I like best: a little ring of lights in the middle of nowhere, with the mechanics working and the next day’s roadbook just handed out.

Marrakech and the Atlas after dark, with the finish bivouac lit.

How it’s made

This one needed a new trick. My other dioramas are a few dozen kilometers across. This one is about 1,600, from Madeira to Figuig and from Seville down past Smara. So it’s built at 1/40 scale and the code scales everything back up, which lets the same camera, water and lighting work. The mountains are stretched nine times taller than life so they read from above. The land and the seabed both come from Terrain Tiles on AWS, which at this zoom include real ocean depths, on a 2 km grid.

OpenStreetMap was too big to pull for a box this size, so the landcover comes from geography instead: green in the north, steppe in between, and desert to the south and in the Atlas rain shadow. The real dune seas, the oases of the Draa, Ziz and Dadès valleys, and the cities are painted on from their real coordinates. The route is read from the same file that drives my tracker page, so when ASO updates the route the diorama follows on its weekly rebuild.

One honest caveat: ASO publishes the towns and the distances, not the tracks. Crews only get each day’s roadbook the night before. So the stages are drawn straight from town to town, with a few extra points to keep stage 1 on land and send stage 4 through Merzouga, as ASO’s notes say. The loop stages are drawn as rings as long as their published specials. Where they actually go is anyone’s guess until January.

As before, I built it with Claude. I brought the rally and the questions, and Claude wrote the code. GitHub Actions builds and publishes it, and the film was rendered there too, split across 20 free computers. The code is on GitHub.
