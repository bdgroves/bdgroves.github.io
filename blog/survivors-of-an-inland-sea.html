🐟 Survivors of an Inland Sea

October 4, 2026 Brooks Groves 11 min read
Science Writing Ecology Nevada Great Basin UNR GBIF

In the early 1990s I was a molecular biology student at the University of Nevada, Reno, working with Dr. Lee Weber on heat shock proteins in fish, including some of the fish in this story. More than thirty years later, I wanted to tell their story properly.

They live in Pyramid Lake, north of Reno: a big blue lake in the desert with no outlet, pale tufa towers along the shore, and white pelicans nesting on Anaho Island. The Paiute name for it, according to the U.S. Geological Survey, is Cui-Ui Panunadu, “fish in standing water.” The people who live there, the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe, are the Kooyooe Ticutta, the cui-ui eaters.

The fish of Pyramid Lake are some of the strangest and toughest in North America. One of them lives nowhere else on Earth. Another was the biggest cutthroat trout ever caught, then vanished, then came back from a mountain creek 300 miles away. To see why they’re here at all, you have to go back 15,000 years.

An inland sea

Lake Lahontan at its highest, drawn by flooding today’s terrain to 1,335 m. Dark blue: what’s left.

At the end of the last ice age, northwestern Nevada was underwater. Lake Lahontan filled the valleys between the ranges with one huge, branching lake. About 15,000 years ago it reached its highest level, 1,335 m above sea level. According to the USGS it covered 22,800 km², slightly more than Lake Ontario, and it was 281 m deep where Pyramid Lake is now. Then the climate dried and it fell fast, between about 14,000 and 13,000 years ago.

I drew the map above by taking a modern elevation model and filling it with water up to 1,335 m, starting from Pyramid Lake. The flood covers 22,314 km², within about 2% of the USGS figure. It’s only an approximation, because the land has slowly rebounded since the weight of the water came off, but it shows how much of Nevada was lake.

Pyramid Lake and Walker Lake are the deepest leftovers. Pyramid is the end of the line for the Truckee River, which flows out of Lake Tahoe, through Reno, and into the desert, where the water has nowhere to go but up. Over thousands of years the lake has become slightly salty, about a sixth as salty as seawater.

When the big lake shrank, its fish were stranded in the pieces. The ones that made it are the native fish of the Lahontan basin.

Five native fishes, every GBIF record in the window. “Inside the old lake” means inside the 1,335 m flood on the map above.

The cui-ui

The cui-ui (say kwee-wee), Chasmistes cujus, is a big lake sucker that lives only in Pyramid Lake. It can grow to about 3.5 kg and live more than 40 years. It’s the last genetically pure member of its genus: the other lake suckers of the West have either disappeared or mixed with other species. Of the 62 GBIF records of cui-ui in the basin, 87% sit inside the footprint of Lake Lahontan, nearly all of them right around Pyramid Lake.

Cui-ui spend their lives in the lake but spawn in the river, swimming up the lower Truckee in spring. That’s where the trouble began.

In 1905 the federal government finished Derby Dam on the Truckee, east of Reno, as the first piece of the Newlands irrigation project. It sent much of the river’s water south to farms around Fallon. Pyramid Lake dropped about 80 feet. As the lake fell, a delta built up at the mouth of the river that the fish had trouble getting over. Next door, Winnemucca Lake, which also had cui-ui, dried up completely; cui-ui lived there until the mid-1930s, and it has been dry since the late 1930s.

The Truckee from Reno to Pyramid Lake, with the three dams that matter most to its fish, and GBIF records of cui-ui and Lahontan cutthroat.

The cui-ui was on the very first federal list of endangered species, in March 1967. Then came the work of keeping it going:

  • A hatchery. Raising cui-ui began in 1973, and the Tribe took it over in 1977. The Koch Cui-ui Hatchery at Sutcliffe opened in 1974, named for Dave Koch, who pioneered raising them.
  • A ladder. In 1976 Marble Bluff Dam and the Pyramid Lake Fishway were built near the river mouth, so fish could get past the delta.
  • Water. The 1990 Truckee-Carson-Pyramid Lake Water Rights Settlement Act called for recovery plans for the cui-ui and the cutthroat, and set up a fisheries fund for the Tribe. The Truckee River Operating Agreement, signed in 2008, took effect in 2015.

It’s working. Since 1991 the adult population has been estimated at 500,000 to 2 million, and spawning runs in 2005, 2006, 2008 and 2010 each topped 200,000 fish. The newest project is a rock ramp at Numana Dam, funded with $8.3 million in 2023, to reopen spawning river that the fish have been cut off from for a century.

The giant trout

The other famous native is the Lahontan cutthroat trout, Oncorhynchus clarkii henshawi. In Pyramid Lake it grew enormous, eating tui chub, which still make up more than half of what biologists catch in their survey nets. In 1925 Johnny Skimmerhorn, a Paiute fisherman, caught a 41-pound Lahontan cutthroat there. It’s still the world record for any cutthroat.

Derby Dam was a disaster for these fish too. They spawn in the river, and with the river cut down and the delta in the way, the last natural spawning run up the Truckee was in the 1940s. The Pyramid Lake cutthroat was gone. Hatchery cutthroat from other Lahontan populations kept a fishery going. The species was listed as endangered in 1970 and moved to threatened in 1975.

Then, in 1977, someone found cutthroat trout in a small stream on Pilot Peak, a lone mountain on the Nevada–Utah border, about 300 miles to the east. The trout expert Robert Behnke thought they looked like Pyramid Lake fish. Decades later, Mary Peacock’s lab at UNR settled it with DNA from museum specimens collected between 1872 and 1911: the Pilot Peak fish were the lost Pyramid Lake lineage, probably carried there long ago by people stocking trout.

The biggest cutthroat trout in the world disappeared from its lake in the 1940s, and turned up again in a creek 300 miles away.

Eggs went to the Lahontan National Fish Hatchery in 1995. The first Pilot Peak fish went into Pyramid Lake in 2006, and by 2014 they were making their own spawning runs. The giants came back: a 31-pound, 4-ounce fish in 2023, and acoustic surveys estimating about a million large cutthroat in the lake in 2019.

Heat, again

This is where my old lab work comes back in. Desert lake fish deal with heat: shallow spawning rivers in summer, warm lake surfaces, low water in drought years. Cui-ui eggs, for instance, survive best between 8.9 and 15 °C, and poorly between 15 and 21 °C.

Heat shock proteins are the cell’s first line of defense against that. When a cell overheats, its proteins start to unfold, and it switches on genes for chaperones like Hsp70 and Hsp27 that grab the damaged proteins and help them refold. Lee Weber and Eileen Hickey’s labs at UNR studied that response in mammal and fish cells, and they were co-authors with Lawrence Hightower’s group on work showing how differently desert fish like the topminnow Poeciliopsis can tune it.

The question has come back around with new tools. In 2008, Peacock’s UNR group heat-tested young Pilot Peak cutthroat and found that how long a fish holds out is partly inherited, and that fish in better condition last longer. In 2019, a team including Peacock scanned the genomes of Pilot Peak trout that had been heat-tested, along with the two remaining native lake populations, Independence Lake and Summit Lake. The genes linked to heat tolerance included ones for protein assembly, the job heat shock proteins do, along with metabolism, heart function and the immune system.

It’s fun to see questions like the ones I was around in the early ’90s still being asked, with tools nobody had back then.

If you visit Pyramid Lake, remember it’s on the Pyramid Lake Paiute Reservation, and you’ll need a Tribal permit to fish or camp. Stop at the fishway at Marble Bluff in spring, when the cui-ui are running. They’ve been making that trip since the inland sea went away.
