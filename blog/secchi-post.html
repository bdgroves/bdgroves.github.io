⚪⚫ They Turned On a Sensor Network at Lake Tahoe. Nobody Wrote the Docs.

September 18, 2026 Brooks Groves 16 min read Updated September 26, 2026

Live dashboard: brooksgroves.com/secchi · Code: github.com/bdgroves/secchi

Hydrology Limnology Lake Tahoe USGS Leaflet Python GitHub Actions

UNR switched on a sensor network around Lake Tahoe, and two days later I started reading it. There was no manual. A week and a half later I had eleven million observations, a lake that looked short of breath (it wasn’t), a forest station nobody can explain, and a long list of things I got wrong. Here’s the project, what it found, and what it taught me.

The project at a glance

What it is
A live dashboard for Lake Tahoe and the forest around it: lake sondes, forest weather and soil stations, and USGS stream gauges, all on one page and refreshed every hour.
Where the data comes from
The Tahoe Environmental Observatory Network (TEON, University of Nevada, Reno) and the U.S. Geological Survey.
How much
About 11.15 million observations, June 2024 to now. That’s every record TEON’s API exposes, kept in a store anyone can clone and query with SQL.
How it runs
Three GitHub Actions workflows. One fetches every hour, one publishes the page, and one watches TEON every six hours and files an issue when something changes.
Go look

A plate on a rope

In 1865 a commander in the Papal Navy asked an astronomer a simple question: how clear is the water?

Angelo Secchi lowered a white plate over the side of a yacht in the Mediterranean and wrote down the depth at which it disappeared.

That’s the whole instrument. A hundred and sixty-one years later it’s still the world standard for lake clarity, and it’s still how Lake Tahoe’s famous clarity gets reported every year. In the late 1960s the disk stayed visible down to nearly 100 feet. In 2025 the average was 69.2 feet. Billions of dollars of restoration work are aimed at closing that gap, and the scorecard is still a plate on a rope.

The disk tells you how clear. It doesn’t tell you why. The why is sediment washing in off the watershed, algae growing in the water, snowmelt, storms, and the soil in between. On September 15, 2026, UNR made the why public: sondes in the lake, data loggers in the forest, cameras on the ridgelines, all reporting every fifteen minutes.

I got my undergraduate degree at UNR. Two days later I started pulling their data.

There was no API documentation. There still isn’t. So I named the project after the plate.

Step one: guess the API

TEON’s website is a JavaScript front end talking to an undocumented backend. Everything I know about it came from watching my browser’s network traffic and poking at URLs until they answered.

The naming convention turned out to be weird in a very specific way. The data endpoints don’t use a sensor’s display name. They use the name chopped down to its first idea:

"Air Temperature & Relative Humidity"  →  /sensors/air-temperature
"Tree stress and growth"               →  /sensors/tree-stress
"Soil Environmental Conditions"        →  /sensors/soil-moisture

That last one isn’t a typo. The soil endpoint is named after one of the things it measures, not what it’s called. All ten sensor types resolved eventually. The last holdout was the oxygen logger, minidot-sensor, which was the one spelling my first guess skipped.

The habit that came out of this ended up being the most useful thing in the project. Don’t write code that assumes a URL. Write a probe: a small command that tries candidate names against the real server and reports which ones answer. Where I did that, I found out fast. Where I skipped it, I paid for it later.

Forty-three sensors, thirteen devices

TEON’s inventory lists forty-three sensors. There aren’t forty-three sensors.

At each forest station, the soil, air-temperature, tree-stress and stream-level endpoints all return slices of one data logger’s table. They have the same record IDs, the same battery voltage and the same row counts. One box, four names.

The giveaway was a column called PTemp_C_Avg: panel temperature, the logger reporting on the inside of its own enclosure. No weather sensor does that. Put it next to the battery columns and an S3 bucket named teon-loggernet-data-storage, and the hardware more or less introduced itself: Campbell Scientific loggers writing to Amazon’s cloud.

Remove the duplicates and forty-three sensors become thirteen physical devices. That’s worth knowing before anyone writes a paper about forty-three independent measurement sites.

One catch took me a while to learn. The slices share rows, but they don’t share columns. Soil comes back with soil fields, air with air fields. Early on I fetched one slice and let it stand in for the rest. That quietly threw away every air-temperature, humidity and tree reading in the history. Same rows, different columns: fetch each one.

The ones you have to swim out to

Two of the lake sondes have Manual in their names, and their records stop abruptly every so often. My first guess was that they’d broken. Wrong. The completeness numbers told the real story before any documentation did:

Sonde Telemetry Records Expected Complete
Blackwood 3manual15,45115,64298.8 %
Meeksmanual15,44115,63298.8 %
4H Camplive52,44354,34496.5 %
Glenbrooklive46,27147,81196.8 %
Sunnysidelive38,56252,42273.6 %

The “manual” sondes are the most complete records in the lake. They log to internal memory and almost never miss a reading. The live ones lose whatever the radio drops. Sunnyside has lost more than a quarter of its record that way.

Then came the detail that settled it. Both manual sondes are short by exactly 191 readings, which is 47.75 hours. Two separate instruments with the same two-day gap isn’t radio trouble. It’s a service visit with both units out of the water at the same time. TEON’s own StoryMap confirmed it:

“Emily Carlson and Katie Senft ride and snorkel to their sensors every month, come rain, shine, and even snow in the winter months.”

Every month, by boat and snorkel, to wipe algae off the optical windows before the readings drift. I think about that every time I look at the dashboard.

Lesson: telemetry buys you timeliness, not completeness. If you want to know what’s happening right now, send it over the radio. If you want to know what happened over the last ten years, a logger somebody visits may give you a better record. The real value of the radio is often just knowing the station is still alive.

The lake that looked out of breath

This is the best finding in the project, and I got the prediction wrong on the way to it.

TEON’s big EXO sondes report dissolved-oxygen saturation of about 83 to 86 %. For a cold, clear, well-mixed alpine lake that’s odd. It reads like a lake that’s slightly short of breath.

Here’s the thing about altitude. Tahoe’s surface sits around 6,227 feet, where the air pressure is only about 80 % of sea level. How much oxygen water can hold depends on that pressure. Measure the percentage against sea-level air and a healthy lake at altitude will look about twenty points low.

So I recomputed saturation from each sonde’s own concentration and temperature, at the lake’s real pressure:

InstrumentSitesPublishedAt lake pressureMean gap
EXO sondes483–86 %104–108 %+21.75
MiniDOT loggers6100–104 %99–104 %−0.41

I expected both instrument families to share the error. They don’t. The little MiniDOT loggers get it right: they agree with my math to within half a point at all six sites, across 265,000 readings. The EXO sondes are the ones set to sea level. Being wrong about that made the finding stronger. It rules out a deliberate network-wide convention, it points at how the EXO units are configured, and TEON’s own MiniDOTs prove the fix is doable.

The lake isn’t short of breath. It’s slightly over-saturated, which is normal in summer, when warming water and daytime photosynthesis push oxygen a little above equilibrium. The dashboard now shows TEON’s number and the corrected one side by side, and it never overwrites theirs. Anything I calculate is labeled “computed from…” so nobody mistakes my number for theirs.

The gremlin gallery

Every monitoring project hits the moment when an instrument says something impossible, and you have to decide whether it’s the world or the wiring. This one had plenty:

  • pH is dead across the whole fleet. Glenbrook reports exactly 0. Sunnyside reports nothing. pH probes are the shortest-lived sensor on a sonde, so this is the most predictable failure in the field.
  • Negative turbidity. Sunnyside sits at −2.11 FNU, and it sits there very steadily, which makes it a misset zero rather than noise. Add 2.11 and it reads about zero, which is right for clear alpine water. A USGS gauge on a nearby creek, a different agency with different hardware, backs that up.
  • Soil pH 1.79. That’s in TEON’s watershed layer for the Cave Rock catchment, and it’s about the pH of battery acid. It doesn’t happen in Sierra granite. My best guess is that map cells with no soil got averaged in as zeros. The dashboard hides it and says why.
  • A photo from the future. One ridgeline camera filed a frame stamped six hours after the snapshot that contained it. The loggers run on Pacific time, the cameras on UTC, and neither says which in the data.
  • A stream gauge that’s honest about itself. The field is literally called Uncalibrated_water_depth, and it reads 0.001 m. The USGS gauge on the same creek agrees it’s essentially dry. Two agencies, two sets of hardware, same answer.

None of this is a knock on TEON. The network had just gone public when I started, and finding problems from the outside, with no obligation to stand behind the numbers, is much easier than running the thing. I’ve sent them one note about the oxygen issue, and a second one is on the way.

Same storm, two shores

The question I most wanted to answer: does the same storm hit the two sides of the lake differently?

Tahoe sits in a rain shadow. The west shore catches Pacific storms first. The east shore sits behind the Carson Range and gets what’s left. Find two stations at the same latitude on opposite shores and you have a natural experiment: same storm, same sun, opposite sides.

Picking the pair took three tries. Blackwood was offline. Glenbrook 2 looked perfect until it turned out to be far wetter than everything around it (more on that in a minute). The right pair was Homewood on the west shore and Glenbrook 5 on the east, both on hillslopes and just 64 meters apart in latitude.

Then TEON’s watershed layer, 60 catchments with 164 attributes each, gave the setup a number:

Homewood      (Madden Creek catchment)     1,463 mm/yr
Glenbrook 5   (Glenbrook Creek catchment)    689 mm/yr
                                           ─────────
                                               2.12×

Twice the rain, on the same line across the map. So does twice the rain reach the soil? Over 376 days of overlapping record:

West (Homewood)East (Glenbrook 5)Ratio
Wetting events27211.29
Total soil wetting146 pts63 pts2.32
Catchment rainfall1,463 mm689 mm2.12

Pretty much, yes. Over the year the west-shore soil took in about 2.3 times as much wetting, against a 2.1 times rainfall difference. And 15 of the 18 storms both stations felt reached the west shore first, which is what you’d expect from weather rolling in off the Pacific.

Now the honest part. An earlier version of this analysis said storms took “+3.7 hours” to cross the lake. Then it said “+11.7 hours“. Both numbers were artifacts. The first came from a matching window that threw out long lags. The second came from storms that started in the evening getting stamped at midnight. Soil moisture swings every day, so events have to be detected on daily averages, and that means most storm arrivals can only be pinned to the day, not the hour. The direction is solid. The crossing time isn’t measurable yet. The ratio also leans on a couple of big west-shore storms, so it’s encouraging, not settled. There’s a rain gauge on the west shore with nearly 600,000 readings that should settle it.

The wet station nobody can explain

Glenbrook 2 is a forest station on the east shore, and it’s soggy. For days I quoted it as 42 % soil moisture against 3.4 % at its neighbors, twelve times wetter. That comparison was one wet site against dry sites on one late-summer afternoon. Over a full year it averages 46 % against 11–16 % next door. It’s still the wettest station by a mile, but three to four times wetter, not twelve. The headline number was an artifact of when I happened to look.

It’s the only forest station with its own stream sensor, so the obvious theory is that it’s wet because it’s next to the creek. I tried three ways to test that, and all three failed in instructive ways:

  1. Correlate soil moisture with stream level. Useless. Rain raises the creek and wets every soil, so everything correlates, and a control site scored higher than the site I was testing.
  2. Correlate only during dry spells after storms. Any two smooth declines correlate. On fake test data, a site hooked directly to a stream barely beat the controls.
  3. Compare how fast each one dries out. The right idea. But the creek and all three soils came back within 0.3 days of each other, which is impossible for four signals that different. Tahoe storms come too close together, and the method was just measuring the length of its own window.

The one thing the data does say is that, day to day, Glenbrook 2 tracks the creek less than its neighbors do. That’s weak evidence against the stream theory. The honest verdict is unexplained. The next step is real GIS work: sampling soil depth, texture and slope right at each station, instead of running another query over data I already have.

A five-digit code that turned out to matter

Late in the build, a USGS parameter code I didn’t recognize showed up at the Upper Truckee River gauge: 70369. I could have guessed from the numbers around it. Instead I wrote a lookup and asked:

70369 — Suspended sediment particles between 0.50 to 16.00 microns, water, unfiltered, computed by regression equation, counts per liter

Half a micron to sixteen microns is exactly the size class that’s legally blamed for Tahoe’s lost clarity. Fine sediment is the main pollutant in the lake’s cleanup plan, which calls for a 65 % cut to bring clarity back to 97.4 feet, the late-1960s average. So that code, at the lake’s biggest tributary, is the regulated pollutant, measured continuously. Its partner 70372 reports the same thing as a daily load, which is the unit the cleanup plan is actually written in.

One catch travels with both of them everywhere on the dashboard. They’re estimates, calculated from turbidity, not particles counted in a lab. A modeled value labeled as a measurement is how you end up trusting a number you shouldn’t.

The day the disk filled

Pulling the full history was a job in itself. My first backfill design rewrote the whole store every time it saved: 1.78 million observations turned into 80 million row writes, about 2 GB of churn to store 45 MB. It filled the disk halfway through a run.

That was bad. What happened next was worse. The save wrote straight over the existing file, so when the disk ran out mid-write it left a half-written file behind, and everything that file used to hold went with it. The cleanup step would have finished the job: it skipped any file it couldn’t read, then deleted it.

A coverage check caught 28,535 missing rows at three lake sites. The next day a better check found the real total, about 76,000. The loss had been there everywhere, but it was only noticed where something was looking. Every row came back from TEON, verified to within six records at every site. Now every save writes to a temporary file and swaps it into place, backfills add data first and tidy up after, and a cleanup that hits a file it can’t read stops and says so.

It watches itself

A lot of what I care about here changes without warning. The manual sondes post nothing until somebody snorkels out. Stations go dark. TEON hides one sonde behind a visibility flag, and the dashboard respects it. If it can’t read that flag, it skips the station rather than guess.

So a watcher runs every six hours. It compares TEON’s inventory against a saved baseline and opens a GitHub issue, which lands in my email, when something real moves. It has already paid for itself: it caught Glenbrook 4 dropping out and then coming back with every reading intact. Digging through the battery data turned up more. Two stations, Blackwood 2 and Glenbrook 2, have gone dark on healthy batteries, so power isn’t the problem. And Glenbrook 1’s battery has read exactly 11.45 volts, over and over, which no real battery does.

What I learned

Here’s the part I’d hand to anyone starting a project like this.

  1. Zero rows looks exactly like no data. Almost every bug I shipped was the same bug in a different outfit: a filter that removed more than I meant it to, and reported success. A USGS request with two statistic codes came back “200 OK” with zero results from every gauge, for hours. A config edit that matched nothing printed “tasks added.” A lookup keyed on the wrong names skipped all 23 sensors without making a single request. None of them crashed. Check the count, not the syntax.
  2. Probe, don’t assume. The project has seven small probe commands, each a few dozen lines, and together they caught six real bugs. The alternative isn’t fewer bugs. It’s the same bugs, shipped, producing numbers that look fine.
  3. When everything misses, look for one mistake. My point-in-polygon test matched 0 of 28 stations. A scattered miss means messy data. A total miss means one systematic error, and here it was TEON’s catchment map, which is stored in meters, not degrees. The fix that finally worked used the one thing I knew for sure: the data should land on Lake Tahoe.
  4. A statistic that can’t tell your idea from the null isn’t weak evidence. It’s no evidence. Glenbrook 2 taught me that three times. Try your method on data where you already know the answer, and be suspicious when unrelated signals give you the same number.
  5. Be suspicious of results that are stronger than you expected. Every correction to the storm analysis moved toward “less dramatic.” Twelve times wetter became three or four. A crisp crossing time became “can’t measure it yet.”
  6. Fake data tests your logic. Real data tests your assumptions. Every analysis here passed its test on made-up data and then ran into something real: timestamps with no time zone, a daily cycle, storms stamped at midnight. The first real run is where you find out what you assumed.
  7. If the store is the only copy, every write has to be safe. That goes double when the only real backup is “the source happens to still have it.”
  8. Corrections are part of the record. I’ve left my wrong numbers in this post on purpose. They’re how you know the right ones were earned.

What’s next

  • A second note to TEON covering the dark stations, the frozen battery reading, the oxygen split with full numbers, and the soil pH layer.
  • Storms against real rainfall, using the west-shore rain gauge, to turn “both stations got wet” into “this much rain made this much wetting.”
  • The trees. A year of dendrometer history just landed, and nobody’s looked at it yet. Tree trunks swell and shrink every day as they take up water. I can’t wait.
  • The cameras. 3,392 frames of Tahoe snowpack from five angles, sitting in a bucket that doesn’t allow public downloads. One friendly email away, hopefully.
  • Ground truth. UC Davis TERC’s Secchi record goes back to 1968. Their work already shows that summer is where clarity is slipping, so any model here has to be seasonal.

To be clear about what this is and isn’t: TERC is already lining up decades of clarity drivers against the Secchi record, with the instruments, the history and the funding. This project isn’t new science. It’s a fast, public, reproducible window onto data that’s otherwise scattered across two agencies and a pile of undocumented endpoints, and it states its own data problems right on the page. That has real value. It’s just a different kind of value.

The personal bit

I went to UNR. I drove around that lake more times than I can count and never once wondered how the clarity number gets made.

It gets made by two researchers in a boat with a snorkel, every month, in the snow, wiping algae off optical windows. It gets made by a plate on a rope that a Vatican astronomer came up with in 1865, which nobody has improved on. And now it also gets made by a dozen or so quiet boxes in the woods and on the water, writing to a bucket in the cloud that nobody documented.

My favorite thing in the whole dataset isn’t a sensor reading. It’s one line in USGS’s description of the Tahoe City gauge:

Datum of gage is 6,220.00 ft above U.S. Bureau of Reclamation datum.

Without that line, the lake level reads 7.07 feet, which means nothing. With it, the surface is at 6,227.07 feet: about four feet above the natural rim and two feet below the legal maximum. Above the rim, Tahoe spills into the Truckee River and on to Pyramid Lake. Below it, the lake stops draining and becomes a terminal lake.

Four feet of margin, hidden in an offset in a station note. The dashboard shows it now, with the rim and the legal ceiling beside it. Because 7.07 tells you nothing, and 6,227.07 tells you where the lake stands in a hundred and twenty years of water law.
