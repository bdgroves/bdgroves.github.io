🍋 The Amalfi Coast in Miniature: Capri, Ravello and the Road Above the Sea

October 29, 2026 Brooks Groves 12 min read
Amalfi Coast Italy Travel Geology History 3D Three.js OpenStreetMap

First it was a little tabletop Seattle, then Yosemite Valley. Both are home ground for me. For the next one I wanted somewhere I simply love, and two places came to mind straight away: Ravello, up on its ridge above the sea, and the island of Capri. They sit at opposite ends of the same stretch of coast, so the choice made itself. Here is the whole Amalfi Coast, in miniature.

It runs from Capri and the tip of the Sorrento peninsula in the west, past Positano, Praiano, Furore, Amalfi and Atrani, to Ravello and Minori in the east. The cliffs come out of real elevation data, the sea sparkles, ferries cross to Capri, boats sit at anchor off the little beaches, and you can ride along the coast road itself. It runs in the browser. Spin it, tilt it from high above down to sea level, and watch the towns light up at night.

Mountains that fall into the sea

What strikes you first about the Amalfi Coast is how little flat ground there is. The Lattari Mountains rise straight out of the sea to more than 4,700 feet, and the towns cling to the gaps where a steep valley meets the water. In the diorama the sea is see-through, so you can watch the land keep falling away under the water: pale sand in the shallows, then turquoise, then deep blue.

The whole coast, with Capri off the tip of the Sorrento peninsula and the Lattari Mountains running east.

🪨 The science

A mountain range made in a warm sea

The rock is limestone. The Lattari Mountains are the backbone of the Sorrento peninsula, the western end of the Campanian Apennines. They are built mostly of limestone and dolomite laid down on the floor of a warm, shallow sea in the age of the dinosaurs, the same kind of rock as a coral island.

Then the mountains rose. As Africa pushed north into Europe and the Apennines were built, those sea-floor layers were lifted thousands of feet and broken by faults. The coast you see is largely a set of fault scarps, which is why the cliffs drop so far, so fast, straight into the sea.

Water carves the rest. Limestone dissolves, slowly, in rainwater, so the coast is full of caves, sea grottos and narrow gorges. The Blue Grotto on Capri and the Grotta dello Smeraldo near Conca dei Marini are both drowned caves.

And the name? “Lattari” comes from the Latin for milk, after the goats that grazed the high pastures. The cheeses still come from up there.

The road above the sea

The coast road, the SS163 Amalfitana, might be the most famous drive in Italy. It’s a narrow ribbon cut into the cliffs, all hairpins and sudden views, with the sea a long way down on one side and the mountain on the other. Hop in with Drive the Amalfi Coast road and you set off east from Positano, past Praiano and the gorge at Furore, around the headlands to Amalfi and on toward Minori. In the diorama it’s about 37 kilometers of road, pulled straight from OpenStreetMap and stitched into one line.

Positano to Furore from the sea, with the coast road along the cliffs and the high road up to Agerola above.

🛣️ History

The Bourbon road

For most of its history the Amalfi Coast was easier to reach by boat than by land. The towns were linked by mule tracks and long flights of stone steps. The road came in the 19th century, under the Bourbon kings of Naples:

  • 1832: Work began on a carriage road along the cliffs.
  • 1835: King Ferdinand II approved the route, and it was named the Strada Ferdinandea in his honor.
  • 1850: The work was finished.
  • 1853: The road was formally opened on January 12.

Today it runs about 50 kilometers, from Meta near Sorrento to Vietri sul Mare. The buses still squeeze past each other on the bends with a few inches to spare, horns going.

Positano, and the boats

Positano tumbles down its hillside to the beach in a stack of pastel houses. John Steinbeck wrote about it for Harper’s Bazaar in 1953, and his line has been on postcards ever since: “Positano bites deep. It is a dream place that isn’t quite real when you are there and becomes beckoningly real after you have gone.”

In the diorama the beach is lined with orange umbrellas in high summer, and the bay is full of boats: yachts and sailboats rocking at anchor, and ferries coming and going from the pier with white wakes behind them. They run the real ferry lines from OpenStreetMap, and stop at the ports before heading back out. Switch the season off and most of the boats and every one of the umbrellas go away.

Positano in high summer: the umbrellas, the ferry at the pier and the boats at anchor.

Capri

Capri sits off the end of the peninsula, a limestone island with cliffs all the way around. Two high points hold it up, Monte Solaro above Anacapri in the west and the ridge of Villa Jovis in the east, with the town of Capri in the saddle between. Off its south-east corner stand the Faraglioni, three great rock stacks rising out of the sea. Every ferry in the diorama seems to be heading for Marina Grande.

Capri, with Monte Solaro on the left, Villa Jovis on the far ridge, and boats at anchor by the Faraglioni.

🏛️ History

An emperor’s island

Capri has been a getaway for a very long time. Augustus took it from Naples in exchange for the bigger island of Ischia, and stayed there often. His successor Tiberius moved there for good in 27 AD and ruled the Roman Empire from the island for the last ten years of his life. He built twelve villas. The grandest, Villa Jovis, sits on the island’s eastern cliffs, and its ruins are still there. Roman writers told wild stories about what went on at Villa Jovis, but many historians now think Tiberius lived a fairly quiet, private life.

🪨 The science

Why the Blue Grotto glows

The Grotta Azzurra is a sea cave on Capri’s north-west coast. The way in is a hole in the cliff only about 2 meters wide and 1 meter high at low tide, so you lie flat in a little rowboat while the boatman pulls you through on a chain.

Inside, the water glows an electric blue. That’s because most of the daylight doesn’t come through the doorway at all. It comes through a much bigger opening below the waterline, and on its way through the seawater the red light is soaked up, leaving the blue. Tiberius is said to have used the cave as a private swimming pool and sea temple. It was famously “rediscovered” for travelers in 1826 by the German writer August Kopisch and the painter Ernst Fries, guided there by a local fisherman, Angelo Ferraro.

Li Galli, the islands of the Sirens

Out in the bay west of Positano are three tiny islands called Li Galli. Legend says they were home to the Sirens, whose songs lured sailors onto the rocks, and Odysseus had himself tied to the mast to sail past them. In more recent times they belonged to dancers: the choreographer Léonide Massine bought the largest one in 1922, and Rudolf Nureyev bought the islands in 1988 and spent his last years there. Today they’re private. You can’t land, but boats anchor around them and people swim in the clear water, just as they do in the diorama.

The Path of the Gods

High above Positano, the Sentiero degli Dei runs along the mountainside from Bomerano, in Agerola, to Nocelle, about 8 kilometers. It’s one of the great walks of Europe, with the whole coast and Capri laid out far below. Italo Calvino described it as a road “suspended over the magical gulf of the Sirens.” In the diorama you can fly to it, then tip the view down and see the coast the way the walkers do.

Lemons on the terraces

Look closely at the slopes and you’ll see they’re striped. The farmers here have been building terraces for nearly a thousand years, stair after stair of dry-stone walls holding up little strips of flat ground on mountainsides that are much too steep to farm otherwise. Many of them grow lemons. In the diorama the orchards and garden terraces get those stripes, rows of small round lemon trees, umbrella pines and scrubby maquis lower down, and chestnut and oak woods up on the mountains.

🍋 Food & farming

The Sfusato Amalfitano

The Amalfi lemon, the Sfusato Amalfitano, is long and pointed, with a thick, fragrant skin and sweet, juicy flesh. It has adapted over centuries to the steep slopes and the sea air.

  • How it got here: lemons reached the coast in the early Middle Ages through Arab trade. Sailors carried them against scurvy.
  • How it’s grown: on terraces held up by dry-stone walls 3 to 7 meters high, built without cement. The trees grow under pergolas of chestnut poles cut in the woods above, and the branches are tied to the frame so the heavy fruit doesn’t snap them.
  • Recognized: in August 2025 the UN Food and Agriculture Organization named the Amalfi lemon gardens a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System.

Most of them end up in limoncello, of course.

Amalfi, the old sea power

Amalfi itself is small today, a town of a few thousand people squeezed into one steep valley, with the great striped cathedral of Sant’Andrea at the top of its stairs. But a thousand years ago it was one of the great powers of the Mediterranean.

⚓ History

The Maritime Republic of Amalfi

  • 839: Amalfi broke away from Byzantine control. It became an independent sea-trading republic, ruled by its own elected dukes from 958.
  • Around the year 1000: At its height, the city and its territory had perhaps 70,000 people. Its merchants traded with Constantinople, Egypt and the whole eastern Mediterranean.
  • The Amalfi Tables: Its code of maritime law, the Tavola Amalfitana, was used by port cities across the Mediterranean and still recognized until 1570.
  • Paper: Amalfi learned papermaking from the Arabs and was one of the first places in Europe to make it. You can still visit an old paper mill there.
  • The compass: Tradition says an Amalfitan named Flavio Gioia brought the mariner’s compass to Europe. Historians doubt he existed, but his statue stands on the waterfront anyway.
  • 1343: A tsunami destroyed the harbor and the lower town. Amalfi never recovered its old power.

In 1997 the whole Costiera Amalfitana was added to UNESCO’s World Heritage list.

Ravello, above it all

Ravello is the one I love most. It sits on a ridge about 1,200 feet above Amalfi and Minori, quiet and green, with two famous gardens hanging over the edge. In the diorama it’s at its best in golden light, with the shadows filling the valleys on either side.

Ravello on its ridge, with Amalfi and Atrani down at the sea on the left and Minori on the right.

🎼 Music & gardens

Villa Rufolo and Villa Cimbrone

Villa Rufolo dates back to the 13th century and the Rufolo family, rich merchants who even turn up in Boccaccio’s Decameron. In 1880 Richard Wagner visited, and was so taken with the gardens that he saw in them the magic garden of Klingsor for his opera Parsifal. Concerts are still held on a stage hung out over the cliff, every summer, in his honor.

Villa Cimbrone, at the far end of the ridge, was remade in the early 1900s by an Englishman, Ernest Beckett, Lord Grimthorpe. Its gardens end at the Terrace of Infinity, a row of marble busts along a balcony with nothing in front of it but sea and sky. Greta Garbo hid out there in 1938. Gore Vidal, who lived just below at La Rondinaia from 1972 to 2004, called the view from that terrace the most beautiful he had seen in all his travels.

Night on the coast

My favorite moment is the end of the day. Slide the clock to Night and the windows light up in Positano, Amalfi and up on the ridge at Ravello, the ferries run lit across the bay, and every boat at anchor shows its little white light on the dark water.

Positano after dark, with anchor lights across the bay.

How it’s made

It’s the same little engine as my Seattle and Yosemite dioramas, with one big new thing in it: the sea. The U.S. Geological Survey data I used for Yosemite stops at the border, so for Italy the land comes from Terrain Tiles on AWS, which stitch together SRTM and EU-DEM elevation, on a 35-meter grid. The coastline comes from OpenStreetMap, and the script uses it to split land from sea cell by cell.

The seabed takes a little make-believe. At this level of detail the elevation tiles are flat at sea level over the water, so I shaped the seabed from each spot’s distance to shore, shelving off steeply the way the real coast does. It looks right, but it isn’t surveyed. The rest comes from OpenStreetMap too: about 45,000 buildings, the roads and paths, the beaches (where the umbrellas go), the orchards (where the lemons go), the ferry lines and the names.

The browser does the drawing in Three.js: the terrain, the layered rock of the slab, the water with its ripples, the boats and their wakes, the cars, and Vesuvius standing on the horizon to the north, with Ischia off to the west. GitHub Actions builds and publishes it all, and the film was rendered there too, split across 20 free computers. As before I built it with Claude: I brought the places and the love for them, and Claude wrote the code. The code is on GitHub.
