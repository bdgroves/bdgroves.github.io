🏳️ Flag Atlas — A Flag Game for the Vexillologically Curious

Brooks Groves
October 2026
HTML · CSS · JavaScript · Wikidata · Wikimedia Commons
Flags Games Maps Cartography Leaflet Open Data Wikidata Pacific Northwest
Play · brooksgroves.com/flags ↗

I've played a lot of flag games. You know the kind: a flag pops up, you pick one of four countries, and somewhere between Chad and Romania you've seen them all. About two hundred flags and you're done. That's plenty for most people. For a flag person, it's the warm-up.

Because the world is full of flags. Every state and province has one. So do Scotland and Sardinia, Bavaria and Bahia, and a surprising number of American cities, some of them wonderful. Switzerland has flags for well over a thousand of its towns and villages. I wanted a game that goes past the countries and keeps going, into flags I've never seen and probably never will, and tells me the story behind each one. So I made one. It's called Flag Atlas, and it has more than 15,000 flags.

It started in Bellingham

Bellingham, up the road from me, has one of my favorite flags anywhere. A blue half circle stands for Bellingham Bay. Three wavy lines are for its waters (the name Whatcom comes from a Nooksack word for noisy water). Two white stars honor the Lummi Nation and the Nooksack Indian Tribe, and the green stripes are for the four towns that merged to make the city. You could draw it from memory after one look, which, as it turns out, is the whole point.

A local designer, Brad Lockhart, made it in 2016. He won a casual design contest run by the Downtown Bellingham Partnership, sold the flag on Kickstarter, handed the rights to the city, and in April 2017 it became Bellingham's official flag. What got him started was a TED talk.

Good flag, bad flag

If you've ever fallen down the city-flag rabbit hole, there's a good chance the same talk gave you the push. In 2015 Roman Mars, host of the design podcast 99% Invisible, gave a TED talk called "Why city flags may be the worst-designed thing you've never noticed." His show had already covered flags in a 2014 episode called "Vexillonaire," and the talk leaned on a little booklet from the North American Vexillological Association, Good Flag, Bad Flag, by Ted Kaye of the Portland Flag Association. The booklet has five rules:

  1. Keep it simple. A child should be able to draw it from memory.
  2. Use meaningful symbolism.
  3. Use two or three basic colors.
  4. No lettering or seals.
  5. Be distinctive, or be related.

Near the end of the talk, Mars put up the flag of Pocatello, Idaho, which a 2004 NAVA survey had ranked the worst of 150 American city flags. It had a trademark symbol on it. The audience laughed. Pocatello took it with good humor: the city formed a committee, invited Mars to come help, and in 2017 adopted a new flag with three peaks, Scout Mountain, Kinport Peak and Chinese Peak, under a bright star. It's in the game, and it's one of my favorite stories in it.

What's in it

Flag of the Day. One flag a day, the same for everyone. Type your guess, and every miss tells you how far off you are and which way to look: "410 mi ↘️." You get six tries, there's a streak to keep, and you can share your result. Each day of the week has a theme. Mondays and Thursdays it's a country, Tuesdays a US state, Canadian province or Mexican state, Wednesdays and Saturdays a region somewhere in the world, Fridays a big city, and Sundays a flag from right here in the Pacific Northwest.

Rounds of ten. Pick a set and play it two ways. Name it gives you four choices, and the wrong answers come from the same country or neighborhood, so you have to really look. Pin it is the fun one: you see the flag and click where you think it flies on a map with no names on it at all, and the closer you land, the more points, up to 1,000 a flag. After every flag you get the place on a map and, where there's one to tell, the story behind the design.

All flags. Every set, searchable, for browsing on a slow evening.

Go deeper. This is the part I made it for. Beyond the countries, all 50 states, Canada's provinces and territories, home nations like Scotland and Wales, about 1,550 regions and 1,600 cities, there's one more level down: more than 12,000 provinces, departments, counties and towns in 50 countries. Pick Italy and you get its provinces, a few of them flying a gonfalon, the tall banner hung from a crossbar. Pick Brazil and there are more than 3,700 flags of its municipalities and regions. Switzerland has nearly 1,700, Japan about 1,400. I will never see most of these flags in person. Now I get to see them anyway.

Flags I'd never really looked at

Some of my favorite discoveries were close to home:

  • Oregon is the only US state with a different design on each side of its flag. Turn it over and there's a gold beaver.
  • Washington has the only green state flag, and the only one with a president on it.
  • Alaska's Big Dipper and North Star were drawn by Benny Benson, who won the territory's flag contest in 1927. For almost a century the story said he was 13; in 2022 historians found he was 14.
  • Salem adopted a new flag in 2022: a blue stripe and a pink cherry blossom with a star cut out of the middle.
  • Spokane's flag, a sun over a blue river, is the city's fourth.
  • Langley, BC flies the crossroads it grew up around, with a blue star for the corner's first name, Innes Corners.

A robot does the heavy lifting

I didn't pick 15,000 flags by hand. A small robot runs on GitHub every hour. Each week it asks Wikidata, the open database behind Wikipedia, for every place with a flag: where it is, how many people live there, and which flag file it flies. It checks each file's license on Wikimedia Commons (every flag in the game credits its artist), pulls the opening of Wikipedia's article about the flag when there is one, and schedules Flag of the Day three weeks ahead.

My favorite part: it also reads the lettering on every flag. A flag that spells out its own name, like "CALIFORNIA REPUBLIC" or "STATE OF OREGON," would be a giveaway, so those sit out the guessing rounds. They're still under All flags. If you've read Good Flag, Bad Flag, that's rule four enforcing itself.

A few gaps, in case you go looking. Seattle's and Olympia's flags didn't turn up in the open data the robot reads, so they aren't in the game yet. And I've left tribal nations' flags out for now. Those belong in it only with each nation's blessing.

Made for me, but come play

I mostly built this for myself, for the evenings when I want to learn what the flag of Piura or Kimberley or Gibsons looks like and why. It isn't a deep code project. It's a fun one. But if you love flags, if you've ever slowed down to look at a city flag on a pole, or you know what a vexillologist means by an SOB (a seal on a bedsheet), give it a go. Start with today's flag, and see how close you can land Bellingham.

Play at brooksgroves.com/flags. The code lives in my site's repository on GitHub. Built in Lakewood, WA, a long way from Pocatello.
