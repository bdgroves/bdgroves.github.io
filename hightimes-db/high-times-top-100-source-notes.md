the bottom of the page. Circled numbers indicate last month's position.

# What the list actually says about 1987–88

## 1. It's a snapshot of a subculture at its most cornered — and its funniest

This is the deepest trough of the Reagan drug war. The magazine's own pages
in these same issues are wall-to-wall urine-testing horror stories, DEA
eradication programs, and Ed Meese. And what do the readers vote for?
"Ed Meese in prison." "Dump Raygun." "Looking forward to Ronald Reagan's
funeral." "Nancy Reagan is a coke slut." "Cop with a flat tire." "Pot is an
herb, Reagan is a dope."

Political rage is the connective tissue of the whole run, but it arrives as
a joke every single time. Nobody writes an essay. They write four words and
mail them to East 43rd Street.

## 2. Ollie North at #1 is the single best artifact here

Late 1987, Iran-Contra hearings just concluded, and readers of the most
notorious drug magazine in America vote a Marine lieutenant colonel who ran
guns and — allegedly — coke for the Contras into first place. Holmstrom
himself is visibly baffled: the cartoon crowd around the border argues
"IT'S A CONSPIRACY!" / "I THINK IT'S FUNNY!" / "I THINK IT'S JUST AWFUL!"

And the footnote gives away the mechanism: *"Hardly anyone voted for anything
that was on the list! That's how Ollie won first place."* With votes scattered
across hundreds of write-ins, a single coordinated bloc — or a single running
joke — takes the top slot. Note that #28 is "Hanging Oliver North." Both
readings sat on the same page.

## 3. It's a pre-internet comment section, and it behaves exactly like one

Everything we now recognize from online culture is here in ink:

- **Brigading and ballot stuffing.** "Equidemius" — a small L.A. band — keeps
  charting, and gets openly called "ballot box stuffer of the month," then
  later "R.I.P." The magazine eventually posts a rule: *one person, one vote,*
  blaming "yuppie types using office equipment + money."
- **Shoutouts to a specific person.** "Priscilla Pancoast (please put this in!
  She reads this mag!)." "Frank + Debby." "Damon + Erin TA — true love always."
  "I love Gayle Martin." These are people using a national magazine as a
  personals column.
- **Deliberate flaming for reaction.** "I hate the Grateful Dead" charts
  repeatedly and drives the editor visibly insane — Ed Hassle's April '88
  column literally asks why those words are "even allowed to be printed."
- **In-jokes that mean nothing to outsiders.** Wonkey + Bonkey. Squiblibian.
  Juan Bonguloid. Frop. Schmee. Gubud. A private vocabulary, mailed in.

## 4. The music tells you exactly where the counterculture was splitting

The old guard holds — Dead, Floyd, Zeppelin, Hendrix, Marley, Zappa,
Beatles, Doors. But punk and metal have arrived and they are *not* being
polite about it: Angry Samoans, Murphy's Law, Bad Brains, The Dickies, Sex
Pistols, The Meatmen, Anthrax, Metallica, Mötley Crüe, W.A.S.P., Iron Maiden,
Stryper, Dead Milkmen, 13th Floor Elevators, Captain Beefheart.

Holmstrom is a punk guy running the column, and you can feel him quietly
enjoying it — the hippie readership and the hardcore readership sharing a
page, occasionally at each other's throats ("I hate the Grateful Dead" vs.
"I'm the #1 Deadhead" in the margins).

## 5. The horticultural shift is right there in the rankings

Watch this progress across fifteen months:

- Jan '87: "Sinse," "Homegrown — six-foot plants in the closet"
- Mid '87: "Indoor homegrown killer green sticky skunk buds (with no seeds),"
  "Indoor Grow Rooms," "Getting $280 per oz. for my homegrown"
- Late '87/'88: "My Phototron," "Chocolate hydroponics," "CO2 + negative ion
  generators," "Indica buds dripping with resin," "Skunk weed flowered with
  bat guano," "Grow Rooms" climbing from 46 to 36

Interdiction pushed importing off a cliff, prices went vertical, and American
readers responded by becoming indoor horticulturists. The Top 100 documents
that transition in real time, in slang, one ballot at a time — and the ads
surrounding the page are all halide ballasts and hydroponic kits.

## 6. The tone would be unpublishable now, and that's part of the record too

Alongside the political satire sits a lot of casual misogyny and crudity
("unliberated women — fuck women's lib," "fat + ugly girls," "no muff is too
tuff"), plus stray Confederate flag boosterism at #69 of one list. It reads
like a men's-room wall with national distribution. Any honest piece about
this feature has to sit with that: it was genuinely funny, genuinely
oppositional, and genuinely ugly in places, often within three entries of
each other.

## 7. The real subject is participation

Every one of these pages ends the same way: three blank lines, #1 #2 #3, and
an address. That's the whole engine. A magazine with half a million readers
handed its back page over to whoever bothered to lick a stamp — no algorithm,
no moderation, a two-month lag between sending a thought and seeing it in
print, and one guy in New York with colored markers counting it all up by
hand and drawing cartoon aliens in the margins arguing about the results.

That's the story: the last analog comment section, and what it looked like
when a subculture under siege got to talk to itself in public.

## What the escalation actually means

This is the part the pages themselves tell you and no secondary source will.
The feature does not simply exist as "the Top 100." It **grows under
reader pressure, in public, over five months**:

**Top 40 → (gap) → "IT'S BACK!" Top 40 → Top 80 "by popular demand" → Top 100**

Holmstrom is visibly being buried in mail. He goes from forty slots to a
hundred in two issues, and the banner headline each time credits the readers
for forcing it. Then, three months after the expansion, he has to print the
ACHTUNG notice ordering one person, one vote — because the volume got big
enough that gaming it became worth someone's while.

That arc is the story in miniature. A back-page filler column becomes the
most participatory thing in the magazine, outgrows its own format twice,
develops a ballot-integrity problem, and lands a Marine lieutenant colonel
at number one — all inside a single year, entirely by mail, counted by hand.

## Also worth noting on the political thread

"Dump Reagan" doesn't arrive with Iran-Contra fatigue in late '87 — it's
already #1 in **February 1987**, and it holds through March as a "landslide."
Iran-Contra broke in November 1986. The readership was voting against Reagan
within three months of the story going public, and stayed there.

Then in November 1987 they put Ollie North at #1 — with "Hanging Oliver
North" at #28 and "Ron Reagan" at #3 on the same page. Nine months of
consistent anti-Reagan voting, then the joke candidate wins because the
vote fragmented. Holmstrom's footnote is the tell: hardly anyone voted for
anything already on the list.

## The ending is the best part of the story

The column doesn't fade out. It **mutates into a fundraiser and then eats
itself.**

In December 1988 Holmstrom converts the ballot into the "NORMLthon": readers
no longer just mail in a vote, they mail in *money* for NORML, and the ranking
is by dollar amount. Nam Myo-Ho Renge Kyo tops the list at $5,000. There's a
whole bottom-corner category for "ENTRIES THAT RECEIVED LESS THAN ONE BUCK."

Through 1989 the two systems run in parallel and the money side wins. By
June 1989 the running total is $3,000 and Holmstrom is openly telling readers
that after the July 4th deadline there's no guarantee
anything makes the list without a donation. He also fires back at critics in
the margin — asking whether people who won't support NORML should be saving
their venom for someone else.

Then December 1989: **THE TOP 500. Total: $9,148.09.** Entries carry both
dollar figures and vote counts. Ed Rosenthal's "Ask Ed" column tops it with
$745.33 from over a hundred votes. And for the first time in the run, the
page is printed in plain black and white — no marker colors, no cartoon
aliens in the border. It reads like an annual report.

That's the arc, end to end, in three years:

**Top 40 → Top 80 → Top 100 → NORMLthon → Top 500**

A free-for-all reader poll grows past its own format twice, develops a
ballot-stuffing problem, gets a one-person-one-vote rule that doesn't hold,
and is finally solved by attaching a price to a vote. The thing that made it
great — anyone with a stamp could put "Wet Monkey Love" at #75 — is exactly
what made it ungovernable, and the fix was to make it cost money.

Whether it survived into the 1990s is outside this archive; the collection
ends with the December 1989 issue.

# Backwards: where it began

| Issue | Format | #1 | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sep 1986 (p. 98)** | **Top 40** | Sinse | **no "last month" column at all — the debut** |
| Oct 1986 (p. 98) | Top 40 | Sex ("the overwhelming favorite") | last-month column appears; mostly dashes |
| Nov 1986 (p. 98) | Top 40 | Sex | column headers get jokey: "HMPH! LAST MONTH'S VOTE…", "THIS MONTH'S EXCITING NEW VOTE! LAST MONTH'S BORING OLD VOTE" |
| Dec 1986 (p. 98) | Top 40 | The Grateful Dead | |
| Jan 1987 (p. 98) | Top 40 | Sex | *in Brooks's collection* |

**The column begins in the September 1986 issue.** The tell is structural: it's
the only installment with no "last month" column, because there was no last
month. Holmstrom adds the tracking column in October, and by November he's
already bored enough with his own format to start writing gag headers over
each column.

## The full arc, September 1986 – December 1989

**Top 40** (Sep 1986) → runs monthly → **gap in spring 1987** → **"IT'S BACK!"
Top 40** (May 1987) → **Top 80 "by popular demand"** (Jun 1987) → **Top 100**
(Jul 1987) → ballot-stuffing crackdown (Oct 1987) → second voting crisis
(Jun 1988) → **NORMLthon** (Dec 1988) → **Top 500**, $9,148.09 (Dec 1989)

Three years and four months, forty slots to five hundred, from a free vote to
a priced one. The first list's #1 was "Sinse." The last list's #1 was a
magazine column that had raised $745.33.

## A note on the debut

Worth noticing what the September 1986 list is *not*. There's no politics in
the top ten — no Reagan, no Meese, no NORML until #34. It's sinse, Budweiser,
sex, Led Zeppelin, pizza with extra cheese, sleeping late. Hulk Hogan at #36.
"High on Life (the cereal)" at #35.

The political fury that defines 1987–88 isn't there at the start. It arrives
with Iran-Contra, and it arrives fast: "Dump Reagan" is #1 by February 1987,
five months after the column began. That's the shortest, clearest version of
what happened to this readership in the late Reagan years, and it's legible
purely from the back page.

# The 1990s answer: it never stopped, it kept changing its name

Source: the High Times Vault (vault.hightimes.com), whose free full-text
search indexes 21,862 articles across 51 years with issue dates attached.
Page images are paywalled ($29.99/yr) but the search index, article titles,
bylines, dates and opening previews are free — enough to trace the column.

## The column's full name history

| Name | Span |
|---|---|
| Top 40 | Sep 1986 – Jun 1987 (with a Top 80 in Jun 1987) |
| Top 100 | Jul 1987 – 1989 |
| NORMLthon / Top 500 | Dec 1988, Dec 1989 |
| **Hemp 100** | by **Jul 1991** – at least **Oct 2000** |
| **Pot 40** | still running **Oct 2007**; ballot still printed **Jan 2012** |

So the answer to "was there anything in the '90s" is: **yes, the whole
decade.** It ran as the Hemp 100 straight through — a Dec 1991 letters-page
complaint references the July '91 Hemp 100; installments appear in Nov 1995,
Feb 1996, Aug 1997; there's a "Halloween Hemp 100" in Oct 2000. It was
prominent enough to be cover-billed and listed in the Grow America
department's monthly contents line for years.

It then became the **Pot 40** — Holmstrom is still bylined on it in October
2007, and a Pot 40 ballot is still being printed in January 2012 (soliciting
votes at hightimes.com by then).

**That's a roughly 26-year run**, 1986 to at least 2012, under four names.

## The other half of the story: Holmstrom's own arc

The Vault bylines track his rise inside the magazine:

- **1986–89** — cartoonist counting ballots by hand ("compiled and drawn by
  John — don't look at me, I just count up the votes — Holmstrom")
- **1991** — writing features: alternative music, Gatewood Galbraith's
  legalize-pot run for Kentucky governor, Quick Flashes news column
- **Dec 1991 / Feb 1992** — signing off as **Executive Editor**
- **Nov 1996** — signing off as **Publisher**
- **1999** — writing "The History of High Times" and a Rob Van Dam feature
- **2014** — still contributing 40-year flashback pieces

The guy who started by tallying votes for "Wet Monkey Love" ended up running
the magazine. And he apparently never handed off the column — he's still
bylined on the Pot 40 twenty-one years after he started it.

## Holmstrom in his own words on the NORMLthon

His December 1989 feature "NORMLTHON '89" (p. 42, 1,187 words) explains the
conversion directly: it began as a one-off way to raise money for NORML by
attaching a donation to a ballot message. The first NORMLthon ran in the
**April 1988** issue — the same issue Brooks owns — and the response was
strong enough that readers wrote in asking him to keep it going. The 1989
edition brought in close to five times the 1988 total.

Note this corrects the earlier reading: the NORMLthon didn't begin in
December 1988. It started as a one-time experiment inside the April 1988
Top 100 and was brought back by demand.
